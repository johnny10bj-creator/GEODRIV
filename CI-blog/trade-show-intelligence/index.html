Trade Show Intelligence: A Field Playbook

Three days on a show floor can compress six months of competitive monitoring — if you go in with a system. Most teams leave with brochures. The smart ones leave with a competitive picture.

Slide 1 of 3

Before the Show: Targeting and Briefing

Intelligence is decided in the two weeks before the flight, not on the floor.

🎯 Build the Stalk List

Pick the competitors and suppliers whose booths you must visit, then rank them. For each, write the open question — new product? new market push? pricing posture? channel changes? — and the evidence that would answer it. A booth visit without a question is a handshake; a booth visit with a question is intelligence.

🗺️ Mine the Show Materials

Exhibitor lists, floor plans, speaking slots, and pre-show press land weeks early. Read the map before you land: a bigger booth than last year is a signal, a move from the hall's edge to the entrance is a signal, a main-stage speaking slot is a signal. Pre-load these observations into your capture sheet before day one, so on-site time goes to verification, not discovery.

📋 Brief the Whole Team

Everyone on the floor is a collector. Hand out a one-page capture sheet: target names, open questions, and photo discipline — booth layout, product tags, spec sheets, staffing. Assign the supplier hall to someone specific. Competitors' suppliers leak more in one conversation than competitors do in a year of press releases.

Slide 2 of 3

During: The Capture System

The goal is evidence, not impressions — and evidence is photographic.

📸 Photograph Everything That Changes

Booth layout, product displays, spec tags, price sheets left on counters, headcount at different hours. A competitor running a two-person booth this year and a six-person one last year has changed strategy — the photo is the proof, and the timestamp is the data. Sort photos into per-competitor folders the same evening, while context is still fresh.

👥 Log Staffing and Demo Behaviour

Which titles work the booth tells you where they're investing: sales only, or applications engineers? Demo scripts reveal positioning — what they lead with is what they think they win on. Log who's staffing, what they demo, and what they deflect. Deflection is positioning data too.

🔧 Recon the Supplier Hall

Your competitors' suppliers know their volumes, their new programmes, and their problems. A structured conversation with a shared supplier can surface a rival's capacity expansion before any press release. Treat the supplier hall as a first-class intelligence target, not a coffee break.

Slide 3 of 3

After: Synthesis Within 72 Hours

Intelligence decays fast — debrief while it's fresh, or it doesn't happen.

⏱️ The 72-Hour Rule

Run the debrief within three days of the show closing. Each collector files their top three findings; photos get tagged to competitors; open questions get owners. After 72 hours, memory blurs and momentum dies — the synthesis becomes a trip report nobody reads.

🧭 Turn Observations Into Positions

The output isn't a photo dump, it's a delta report: what changed versus last year, what it means, and what you'll do about it. Booth size up + applications engineers on the floor + a new demo script = a market push. Write the implication, not just the observation.

📚 Feed the Standing CI System

Fold the findings into continuous monitoring: new products trigger launch tracking, new distributors trigger channel watch, supplier rumours get verified through other channels. A trade show is a burst sample of a competitor's state — the value comes from connecting it to the continuous picture, not from the show itself.

The bottom line: Trade shows are the only venue where competitors and their suppliers voluntarily gather in one hall and answer questions. Most teams convert that advantage into brochures. The teams that convert it into a structured competitive picture — before, during, and after — compress months of monitoring into three days.

Your competitors will be at the next show with a plan.

Go with one too.
