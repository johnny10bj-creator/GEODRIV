The Threat of Capacity Is Capacity

In industrial markets, an announced intention changes behavior years before it changes output. The intelligence value of a capacity signal is not the fact itself; it is what the fact makes other people do.

The argument: An expansion announcement reorders supplier queues, customer purchasing plans, and competitor pricing posture before the first product ever ships. Reading intent signals is therefore not speculation about the future. It is observation of the present, because expectations are where industrial markets actually transact.
The case

The case that made this obvious

As reported and analyzed by the Financial Times, an aerospace manufacturer drew up plans to produce its own turbine blades and guide vane components, parts that had become scarce across the industry. The analysis highlighted a mechanism worth stealing: even if the manufacturer never ramps to true mass production, the mere credible capability changes the math for incumbent suppliers. If those suppliers believe the customer can make the parts itself, they have a stronger incentive to expand their own capacity, which in turn eases delivery queues that currently stretch toward the end of the decade, around 2030.

Notice what moved first in that account. Not a factory, not a production line, not a single shipped part. What moved was the suppliers' incentive structure, on the strength of an intention backed by visible preparation. The announcement did economic work before any capacity existed.

This is the general shape of signaling in industrial markets. The factual content of a signal is almost secondary. What matters is which expectations it revises, and whose behavior those expectations control.

The mechanism

Why expectations move first

Industrial markets run on commitments made long before output exists. Customers lock capacity and delivery slots years ahead because qualification and tooling take that long. Suppliers expand lines against expected demand because equipment lead times punish late decisions. Competitors adjust pricing and configuration against what they believe the supply situation will be, not what it is today.

In that environment, an expectation is not a mood. It is an input to signed contracts, capex approvals, and quoted lead times. A credible intention is therefore a present-tense market force. The firm that only tracks realized capacity, plants built, lines running, units shipped, is reading the scoreboard of a game whose moves were made two or three years earlier.

The reason expectations bind so tightly is time. A qualification cycle for a safety-relevant component can take years. Tooling and capex approvals add their own calendars. In markets built on that kind of lag, waiting for certainty is itself a decision, and usually the most expensive one available, because the capacity you will need in three years has to be claimed today. Everyone with exposure to the queue is therefore forced to trade on intentions, whether they call it that or not.

The practical consequence inverts the usual analyst instinct. Trained skeptics discount announcements as talk and wait for hard evidence. In industrial markets the talk is the early evidence, provided the talk is backed by observable preparation. That preparation is exactly what can be read systematically, which is where the next section goes.

The signals

Intent signals in industrial markets

Intent leaves fingerprints in public records long before output appears. Each signal below is readable, and each moves a specific audience:

  • Certification activity and standards-committee participation. Joining a committee or starting a certification process commits money and senior time. It tells customers to begin qualifying you, and it tells incumbents that a new entrant is paying real costs.
  • Pilot and trial production lines. A pilot line is a capability demonstration at small scale. It moves suppliers' beliefs about whether you can make the part, which is precisely the mechanism at work in the aerospace case.
  • Equipment tenders and purchase announcements. Machine orders carry long lead times and are hard to fake. They move competitors' timelines for your entry.
  • Requests for quotation sent to upstream suppliers. The volume and specification inside an RFQ reveal intended scale. They move the upstream supplier's own capacity planning.
  • The geography of patent filings. Filing in a jurisdiction costs money per country. Where a competitor files is a map of where it intends to sell or manufacture.
  • The type of hiring, not the volume. Process engineers, automation specialists, and quality-system roles signal production intent; sales hiring signals market entry. It moves rivals' read of which stage you are in.
  • Customer announcements and joint development agreements. A named customer commits both sides publicly. It moves other customers' willingness to engage and financiers' willingness to fund.

None of these signals is secret. All of them are legible to anyone who reads systematically, which is exactly why they function as signals at all.

The discipline

Using it without over-reacting

Intent signals are not capability. A signal can be sincere and still fail on execution. It can also be tactical, issued precisely to make you expand, hesitate, or reprice. Bluffing is cheap compared with building, and every experienced industrial player knows it. Treating every announcement as destiny is how signal-reading turns into being played.

The filter is a three-step test. First, is the signal verifiable through costly preparation, meaning equipment, certifications, hiring, and filings, rather than words alone? Second, whose behavior has already changed in response: are suppliers reallocating, customers requalifying, competitors repricing? Third, what is our smallest reversible response, the move that keeps us positioned if the signal is real but does not strand us if it is theater?

Then put the signal where it belongs: into scenario planning, not into conclusions. A credible expansion intent becomes a scenario with a probability and a tripwire, a defined observation that will raise or lower the probability next quarter. The output is never "they will build it." The output is "their intent has already changed these three behaviors, and here is our pre-committed response if the next tripwire fires."

One operational note makes this stick: assign each live intent signal an owner and a review date. Signals that nobody owns get debated once and forgotten; signals with an owner get re-scored on schedule, and their tripwires get checked against fresh observation. The discipline is boring, and it is the difference between reading signals and collecting headlines.

The signal is already changing behavior. The question is whether you saw it.
