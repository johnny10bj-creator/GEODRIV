Tracking Competitors Through Their China+1 Moves

Capacity relocation is not an announcement. It is an 18-36 month observable process, and every step leaves traces that appear before the press release.

The stance: A competitor's China+1 move is a sequence, not an event. Company registration, hiring, equipment tenders, certifications, customer qualification, and customs patterns all shift in a readable order, and each shift precedes the official statement it eventually justifies. Teams that read the sequence get one to two years of lead time. Teams that wait for the announcement get a fait accompli.
The reality

What China+1 actually looks like from the outside

From the outside, China+1 almost never looks like a factory closing. Most manufacturers hedge: they add a second site while keeping the first running, because existing customer programs, molds, and certifications are tied to the original location and cannot move quickly. The move you need to track is the build-up of the new site, which starts long before anyone uses the word relocation.

This is good news for intelligence work. A hedge is slow, capital-intensive, and administratively heavy. Every stage of it touches a registry, a job board, a tender platform, or a shipping lane. The statement comes at the end; the sequence comes first. Your job is to read the sequence.

The mistake to avoid is treating any single trace as proof. A new legal entity might be a sales office. A hiring burst might be a service team. Only the order and accumulation of traces separates a real capacity build from administrative noise, which is why the sections below are arranged chronologically.

One more feature of the hedge pattern matters for reading it: the new site is almost always sized for a specific customer or program, not for general capacity. Companies do not build speculative plants in a second country. They build because one or two anchor customers demanded regional supply. This means the sequence is not only a map of what the competitor is doing; it is a map of what their customers demanded, and those customers are often yours as well.

The sequence

The observable sequence, in order

  • Local company registration and land or industrial-park filings. The earliest trace of all. A new legal entity in the destination country, followed by land-use or park-occupancy publications, appears before any physical activity. Earliest visible in: corporate registries and park authority publications.
  • Hiring advertisements. Role mix tells the story. Plant managers, quality managers, and process engineers appear first; operator hiring comes last, closest to production start. Earliest visible in: local job boards and the company's own careers page, filtered by location.
  • Equipment tenders and logistics traces. Machinery procurement, rigging and heavy-transport bookings, and utility connections all precede installation by months. Earliest visible in: tender platforms, dealer activity, and freight or customs records for capital equipment.
  • Certifications and customer audits. A quality-system certificate for the new legal entity, then customer audits, then supplier codes. No customer ships production volume from an unaudited site. Earliest visible in: certification registries and supplier-quality job postings.
  • Customer announcements and joint development. Dual-source qualification language, co-development announcements, and customer-side references to regional supply. Earliest visible in: the customers' communications, not the competitor's.
  • Customs and export-destination changes. New origin ports, shifting destination mixes, and changing shipment volumes by trade lane. This is the confirmation layer: by the time trade data moves, the site is producing. Earliest visible in: shipment records.
  • Local supply-chain build-out. Suppliers following the competitor: packaging, tooling, resin or material distributors setting up nearby. When the supply chain moves, the move is structural. Earliest visible in: the suppliers' registrations and hiring, which are less guarded than the competitor's own.
The interpretation

Hedge or real migration: reading it correctly

The same sequence can mean two very different things, and the response each demands is different. Four criteria separate a hedge from a genuine migration.

Original capacity. In a hedge, the home site keeps investing: maintenance capex continues, hiring continues, machines are replaced. In a real migration, home-site maintenance spending falls, hiring freezes, and equipment starts leaving. Watch the home site as carefully as the new one.

Service and support network. A hedge duplicates production but keeps engineering and service anchored at home. A real migration moves application engineering, tooling support, and after-sales capability to the new region, because customers there will demand it.

Certifications. Duplicated certifications, with both sites kept current, mean a hedge. Certifications transferred or allowed to lapse at the original site mean the programs themselves are moving.

Key personnel. The sharpest signal of all. When senior process engineers, mold or tooling leads, and site quality managers relocate personally, the company is moving capability, not just capacity. People are the last thing hedged and the first thing migrated.

Apply the four criteria together rather than separately. Any one of them can mislead: a home-site hiring freeze can be a cost program, and a duplicated certification can be customer requirement. But when home investment falls, service moves, certifications transfer, and key engineers relocate in the same twelve months, the hedge reading stops being defensible. The discipline is to write down which reading you currently believe, and which single observation would force you to change it.

The response

What to do with it

There are three legitimate readings of a competitor's China+1 sequence, and each carries one immediate action.

  1. Direct threat. The new site puts the competitor inside your customers' tariff-safe or regional supply requirements, and they will be invited to quote programs you currently hold. Immediate action: update your target account list and reprioritize the accounts most exposed to the new geography before the RFQs arrive.
  2. Customer-structure opportunity. Customers who push a supplier to build a second site are, by definition, dual-sourcing. A customer mid-transition is a customer with an open qualification slot. Immediate action: approach the accounts being transitioned while qualification is open, because that window closes once the new site is approved.
  3. Supply-chain risk. If the competitor's suppliers follow them, your own suppliers may follow too, and your delivery radius and lead times shift underneath you. Immediate action: identify which of your critical suppliers serve that competitor, lock capacity or terms with them, and re-check your own delivery radius against the new geography.
